Soccer · Never Give Up

The Kick
That Missed

Leo could hear his heart louder than the crowd. The score was tied, the clock had run out, and now everything came down to one penalty kick. His kick.

He placed the ball on the white spot and stepped back. Five steps, just like Coach Rivera had taught him.

“You’ve practiced this a hundred times,” he whispered. “Just one more.”

The whistle blew. Leo ran, swung his leg, and—

The ball sailed over the crossbar.

For a moment, everything went silent. Then came the groans from the bleachers — and a sound Leo would remember for a long time: the other team cheering.

He stared at the grass and wished he could disappear.

That night, Leo sat on his bed with his cleats still on. There was a knock at the door. It was his older sister, Ana.

“Rough game?” she asked.

“I lost it for everyone,” Leo said. “I’m never taking a penalty again.”

Ana sat next to him. “You know what Coach told me when I struck out in the championship game? Missing doesn’t make you a loser. Quitting does.”

Leo thought about that for a long time.

The next morning, before school, Leo was at the park with a bag of balls. One kick. Then another. Then fifty more.

By Saturday his leg ached — but his kicks were landing exactly where he aimed.

of Brave

Maya was the fastest player on the Hornets. Fast feet. Fast hands. Fast passes. But there was one thing she couldn’t do fast at all…

…speak up.

When Coach Bell asked who wanted the last shot, Maya’s hand stayed glued to her side. Every time. Her voice just hid somewhere deep inside her sneakers.

Then came the final game of the season. The Hornets were down by one point, and the gym was so loud the floor shook. Coach Bell called a time-out and looked at every player.

“Ten seconds left. Who wants the last shot?”

The Last Lap

SwimmingPersonal best

Sam was always the last one out of the pool. Not because he loved swimming the most — because he was the slowest.

At every meet, he’d touch the wall and look up to see the other swimmers already wrapped in their towels.

“Why do I even bother?” he asked his mom one evening.

She handed him a small notebook. “Stop racing them,” she said. “Start racing you.”

Sam opened it. On the first page, in his mom’s neat handwriting, it said: My Times.

The next day he swam his four laps, climbed out, and wrote a number on the very first line: 54 seconds. It didn’t look like much. But it was a start.

So Sam started writing down his times. Every practice. Every lap.

Week by week, the numbers began to shrink. 52 seconds. 50. 48.

Stop racing them.
Start racing you.

At the final meet, Sam finished last again. But when he looked at the board, he grinned so wide his goggles fogged up: 46 seconds. His best time ever.

Nobody else noticed. But Sam did — and that was the only race that mattered.
